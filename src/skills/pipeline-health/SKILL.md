# Pipeline health

Give a sales leader a grade that agrees with their gut when their gut is right, and argues with it when it isn't. Every grade comes with the reasons and the specific deals that would move it.

## What you need

- **The open deals.** For each one, its value, stage, expected close date, when it last changed, and anything known about fit or the client's recent replies.
- **History.** Past pipelines and what they actually turned into, or at least win rates and typical time to close by stage. Use the team's own numbers, never industry averages.
- **The budget.** The revenue target for each month the deals are expected to land in.

If history is thin, say so at the top and keep going. A grade with an honest warning is more useful than no grade.

## Grade the forecast first

Build the grade around one question. How much revenue has a pipeline like this historically turned into, compared with the budget for the month it lands in?

1. For each open deal, estimate the revenue it will likely produce from its value and the team's historical conversion for its stage.
2. Add those up by the month each deal is expected to close.
3. Compare each month's expected revenue with that month's budget.
4. On budget is an A. Well short is a D or F. Say what ratio you used for each letter, so the user can adjust it.

Count each shortfall once. If the forecast is already low because deals are early, don't also mark the grade down separately for stage mix.

## Then check what's underneath

An A also needs a healthy pipeline. Compare each of these with the team's own history, and let weak areas pull the grade down.

- **Volume.** Are there enough deals?
- **Fit.** Do the deals look like the ones the team usually wins?
- **Value.** Are deal sizes in the normal range, or propped up by one or two big ones?
- **Stage spread.** Is there enough coming up behind the deals closing soon?
- **Freshness.** How many deals haven't moved in longer than usual? A pipeline full of stale deals is never healthy, however big it looks.
- **Client signals.** Are clients replying, and how do their replies read? If you judge tone, use plain lists of phrases the user can read and change. Don't use a black box.

Only use a measure if you can show it has related to results in this team's history. Leave out the ones you can't check, and say you left them out.

## Write it up

Keep it critical but helpful. The point is to show where to focus, without scaring anyone.

1. **The grade**, in one line with the expected revenue against budget.
2. **The reasons.** Two to four, most important first.
3. **The odds of reaching budget**, with the history they're based on.
4. **Improve.** A short list of specific deals to work on, what to do with each, and roughly how much each step would move the forecast. Start with the stale and stuck deals.
5. **Limits.** How much history the forecast is fitted on, and how strong the fit is. If it's weak, say so plainly.

## Check it later

When the month arrives, compare the forecast with what actually closed and note how far off it was. Use that to tune the grade. Don't keep a model that keeps missing.

Keep the data where it is. None of this needs a deals list to be sent anywhere else.
