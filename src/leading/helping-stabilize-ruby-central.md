In July 2026, I stood up at RubyConf to talk about the year Ruby Central had just had. Everyone in the room knew it had been a hard one, and I didn’t want to pretend otherwise. I acknowledged the difficult year and the uncertainty that was still ahead. I thanked the community. And I tried to keep the focus where it belonged, on people. Ruby’s strength has always come from its developers, maintainers and contributors, and from the relationships between them, far more than from any institution, including the one I was there to represent.

By the last day of the conference, I was asking people to do more than listen. Come by the Ruby Central booth. Learn about the steering committees. Volunteer. Turning goodwill into people who show up turned out to be harder than it sounds, and that lesson stayed with me.

Getting to that stage took most of a year, and a lot of work that happened far from any stage.

## An organization everyone leans on

Ruby Central does work that most Ruby developers rely on without thinking about it. It runs RubyGems.org, where Ruby’s packages are published and installed, and it puts on RubyConf.

When I first talked with Ruby Central about joining its board, in late 2025, the organization was in the middle of a public controversy. People were arguing about the stewardship of RubyGems and Bundler, about who had access to which repositories, and about how decisions were being made.

I saw an organization doing critical work for the Ruby ecosystem, facing serious questions about how it operated, whether people trusted it, and what its future looked like. I joined the board in January 2026 and became its President.

My involvement went past the usual board responsibilities almost immediately. To help, I needed to understand the finances, the legal situation, the governance, and the relationships with sponsors and the community, all at the same time. Each of those problems made the others harder.

## The math

Early in 2026, Ruby Central’s financial situation was serious enough to threaten its future. The cash on hand would only last a few more months. Funding the organization was counting on was delayed. Some longtime supporters had stepped back or were thinking about it. And most of the money came from a small number of companies, at the exact moment the controversy made new supporters hard to find.

Events were part of the risk. A large conference can lose a lot of money, and an organization that depends on events to fund its mission is always one bad year away from trouble.

Cutting costs would help, but it wouldn’t be enough on its own. Ruby Central needed a more reliable way to pay for its work. So I worked with the board on a plan with two parts. Stabilize the finances right away, and build a broader, steadier base of support for the long term.

## The Ruby Alliance

A big part of the answer was the Ruby Alliance, a coalition of companies making financial and engineering commitments to the long-term health of Ruby.

The idea goes further than a logo on a sponsor page. Members contribute money and technical participation. They get shared visibility into infrastructure priorities, and they take part in regular conversations about the ecosystem’s health, its risks and its roadmap.

We were careful about one thing in particular. The Alliance gives companies a real way to support Ruby without giving any single company control over Ruby Central or the infrastructure it runs. Sustainable support and good stewardship had to come together, or the whole thing would just trade one kind of risk for another.

In May 2026, [thoughtbot](/leading/building-the-next-chapter-of-thoughtbot/) announced it was joining the Ruby Alliance. Today, the Alliance is one of the main reasons Ruby Central is financially stable.

## A working board

The other part of the answer was changing how Ruby Central runs.

In June 2026, I described publicly where we were heading. Toward a volunteer working board, lower costs, and a focus on the programs that matter most. That meant cutting overhead we could live without. It meant a board whose members contribute directly to the work. It meant protecting the critical infrastructure and RubyConf while operating with fewer resources. It meant putting financial accountability and transparency at the center of our decisions. And it meant making room for volunteers and companies to contribute work as well as money.

Those efficiencies, together with the Alliance, are why Ruby Central is on stable footing today.

## Facts and allegations

Ruby Central also faced a legal threat, rooted in the disputes over RubyGems, Bundler and former maintainers. It raised hard questions about access, responsibilities, governance and authority, and it carried real financial risk for an organization that couldn’t afford much more.

I kept coming back to one principle. Separate what’s documented from what’s alleged. I didn’t want Ruby Central making admissions it couldn’t support, or accepting unreasonable demands just to make a public controversy go away.

I also didn’t want us to hide behind that principle. Some of the criticism pointed at real shortcomings in our governance and our processes, and those needed fixing whatever happened with the legal side. Defending the organization’s legitimate interests and admitting its mistakes can feel like opposite instincts. A good outcome needed both.

I pushed for urgency, and for talking to people directly. Lawyers and public statements have their place, but they don’t rebuild trust between people on their own. Conversations do.

The legal threat is now resolved, and with it, one of the biggest sources of uncertainty hanging over the organization.

## When the attack came

Along the way, the Ruby gem ecosystem was hit by a security attack.

This is exactly the kind of moment Ruby Central exists for. Its job is to keep the infrastructure that distributes Ruby packages trustworthy, and an attack on the software supply chain goes straight at that trust. The packages that move through RubyGems.org end up in developers’ projects, in businesses, and in production systems around the world.

Ruby Central navigated the attack and came through it. For an organization still rebuilding its finances and its credibility, it was a test of whether it could do the most important part of its job under pressure. It could.

## Rules that last

Fixing the money and the legal exposure would only matter if the organization was built to avoid the same problems next time. So we’ve been modernizing how Ruby Central is governed, starting with its bylaws.

The work focuses on making fiduciary responsibility clear, strengthening financial oversight, and spelling out what officers and committees are responsible for. We’ve worked on staggered board terms so the board doesn’t turn over all at once, on safeguards against conflicts of interest, and on clearer relationships between Ruby Central and the technical communities it serves.

One distinction mattered to me more than most. Community participation should be meaningful, without creating competing centers of legal authority. Steering committees can advise, organize and contribute a great deal. The board keeps responsibility for the nonprofit’s finances, its legal obligations and its decisions as an institution. When everyone knows where that line is, people can contribute without wondering who’s really in charge.

## Where things stand

Less than a year after I joined the board, Ruby Central is financially stable, thanks to the Ruby Alliance and a leaner way of operating. The legal threat is resolved. The organization came through a security attack on the infrastructure it runs. And its governance is on firmer ground, with a working board and clearer rules about who is responsible for what.

There’s more to do. We’re still finding the best ways for the community to take part, through steering committees and volunteering. And we’ve been exploring ideas that could help Ruby Central do more than react, like apprenticeships to bring new contributors in, individual memberships, and broader ways for companies to support the work.

## What I believe now

A crisis is rarely one problem. Ruby Central’s financial, legal, security and trust problems were all connected, and fixing any one of them meant working on the others at the same time. Trying to solve them in order would have taken longer than the organization had.

Institutions are made of people. What’s worth protecting in an organization like Ruby Central is the community it serves, and the way to protect it is to talk to people directly, early, and honestly.

Being honest about mistakes and standing up for what’s right can happen together. The organizations people trust most do both.

And something this important to so many people shouldn’t depend on one good conference or a handful of generous companies. Spreading the responsibility across many companies and many people is slower to build, and much harder to break.

For the first time in a long while, Ruby Central gets to spend its energy on what comes next for Ruby.
